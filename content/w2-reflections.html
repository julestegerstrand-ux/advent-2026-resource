Waiting with Peace: Prepare the Way

The theme for the second week of Advent is Peace. Peace can feel like an impossible dream as we witness violence, injustice, dehumanization, and suffering often justified in the name of God. We long for peace, but we know that peace is more than the absence of conflict. Peace is more than silence, stability, and the appearance of order. Sometimes what the world calls peace is simply the quiet that comes when those who suffer are told to remain silent.

Toward the end of his Letter from Birmingham Jail, Dr. King notes:

"I must make two honest confessions to you, my Christian and Jewish brothers. First, I must confess that over the last few years I have been gravely disappointed with the white moderate. I have almost reached the regrettable conclusion that the Negro's great stumbling block in the stride toward freedom is not the White citizens' “Councilor” or the Ku Klux Klanner, but the white moderate who is more devoted to “order” than to justice; who prefers a negative peace which is the absence of tension to a positive peace which is the presence of justice; who constantly says “I agree with you in the goal you seek, but I can't agree with your methods of direct action” who paternistically feels that he can set the timetable for another man's freedom; who lives by the myth of time and who constantly advises the Negro to wait until a “more convenient season.” Shallow understanding from people of good will is more frustrating than absolute misunderstanding from people of ill will. Lukewarm acceptance is much more bewildering than outright rejection."

King's words challenge us to consider what kind of peace we are waiting for. Are we waiting for a peace that preserves the comfort of those who already have power, or are we longing for the kind of peace that emerges when justice is made real? Advent does not invite us to settle for the appearance of peace while our neighbors continue to suffer. It calls us to prepare the way for the coming of Christ, whose presence disrupts the status quo and invites us into a transformed world. Preparing the way means more than waiting quietly for God to act. It means examining the paths we have created, confronting the obstacles that keep people from experiencing dignity, and participating in God's work of making things right. The peace for which we wait is not the absence of conflict, but the presence of God's justice, compassion, and restorative love.

The prophet Isaiah speaks to a people who have experienced devastation, displacement, and exile. Into their suffering, God declares, “Comfort, O comfort my people” (Isaiah 40:1). These words are not spoken to a people whose circumstances are already peaceful. They are spoken to people who need to know that their suffering has not been forgotten and that their story is not over. God promises that valleys will be lifted, mountains and hills made low, and rough places made plain. The vision is comforting but also disruptive. The world itself must change as God prepares the way for redemption.

This is the peace of God. Peace does not ask the vulnerable and the suffering to accept their circumstances. Peace leans toward a world transformed by God's righteousness and justice. The coming of God brings comfort to those who suffer and challenges the conditions that produce that very suffering. God's peace does not depend on pretending that everything is well. It begins with the promise that God has not abandoned us.

The writer of 2 Peter reminds us that God's timing is different from our own. What feels like delay is not God's absence. We wait for “new heavens and a new earth, where righteousness is at home” (2 Peter 3:13). While we wait, we are called to live lives of holiness. Waiting for God's peace is not passive. It shapes how we live now. We practice peace by refusing to participate in dehumanization, by confronting injustice, by extending compassion, and by building communities where our neighbors are treated as beloved children of God.

In Mark's Gospel, John the Baptist appears in the wilderness, proclaiming a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins. His message echoes Isaiah's call to prepare the way of the Lord. Before Jesus begins his public ministry, the people are invited to turn around, to change direction, and to make room for God's coming reign. The way of peace requires preparation. It asks us to examine the paths we have followed, the evil we have tolerated, and the ways our lives and communities must change to reflect God's vision.

As we wait during this second week of Advent, may we resist the temptation to confuse peace with comfort or silence. May we hear God's promise of comfort for those who suffer. May we live faithfully in the time between the promise and its fulfillment. And may we prepare the way of the Lord by becoming people who embody the peace we proclaim. Amen.
