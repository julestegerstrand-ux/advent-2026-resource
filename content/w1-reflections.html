Waiting with Hope: Keep Awake

Advent begins in the dark. This may seem like a strange way to begin the season. We are surrounded by lights. Christmas music is playing. Decorations are going up. Stores are filled with reminders that Christmas is coming. We light candles, hang lights, and decorate our homes because we are preparing to celebrate the coming of Christ.

And yet, the church begins Advent with Scriptures that refuses to ignore the darkness.

Isaiah cries out, “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down.”

The psalmist pleads, “Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved.”

Jesus speaks of the sun being darkened, the moon losing its light, and the stars falling from heaven.

And then Jesus says, “Beware, keep alert." And, "Keep awake.”

This seems like an odd way to begin preparing for Christmas. But perhaps this is exactly where Advent needs to begin. The Christian hope is not born from pretending that everything is fine. Authentic hope tells the truth about the shadows while trusting that God is still at work.

Isaiah 64 comes from a people who know something about the shadows. Israel has returned from exile, but the return has not produced the restoration they imagined. The people are home, but home is not yet what it was supposed to be. Their restoration remains incomplete.

And so Isaiah prays with astonishing honesty, “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down.” This is not a polite prayer. Isaiah is asking God to show up. Isaiah is asking, "God, where are you?" He is crying out, "God, we need you here," Perhaps even, he's shouting, "God, we need you to come down into the mess we are living in."

And then Isaiah remembers who God is: “Yet, O Lord, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand.” The people are waiting. But they are waiting with hope. Their hope does not ignore of diminish the darkness. Their hope is that the darkness is does not have the last word. That is an important distinction.

There are moments when Christian hope can become a way of avoiding reality. We can use hopeful language to rush past grief, injustice, disappointment, or pain. We can tell people to “just have faith” when what they really need is someone willing to sit with them in the darkness.

Isaiah does something different. Isaiah names the darkness. He cries out. He tells the truth. And yet, Isaiah still hopes.

That is the posture of Advent. We don't have to choose between honesty and hope. We can look directly at what is broken and still believe that God is not finished. The world is still filled with shadows.

We see them in wars that destroy communities and families. We see them in poverty and displacement. We see them in racism and injustice. We see them in addiction and abuse. We see them in sickness and grief. We see them in families carrying burdens that nobody else can see. We see them in the loneliness that can become especially painful during a season that constantly tells us we should be happy. And sometimes the shadows are much closer to home.

Some of us are entering Advent carrying grief. Some are waiting for a diagnosis. Some are waiting for reconciliation. Some are waiting for a job. Some are waiting for a child to come home. Some are waiting for something in themselves to change. Some are simply tired. Advent does not ask us to deny those realities.

Instead, Advent teaches us how to wait in the midst of them. That is why Jesus' words in Mark are so important: “Keep awake.” Jesus has just described a world filled with despair. This includes war, persecution, deception, suffering, and cosmic disorder. And then he tells his disciples to stay awake. He doesn't tell them exactly when the Son of Man will come. He doesn't give them a chart that allows them to calculate the end.

Stay awake. Pay attention. Remain attentive. Don't become numb. Don't fall asleep to what God is doing. Don't allow the darkness around you to convince you that God has stopped working.

The command to keep awake is therefore not primarily about predicting the future. It is about learning how to hope and embody the Gospel in the present.

We wait for Christ by becoming attentive to Christ. But Christian attentiveness cannot stop with seeing.

To keep awake is to become awake to the suffering of our neighbors. And once we are awake to suffering, Christian discipleship asks us what we will do about what we have seen.

Catholic theologian M. Shawn Copeland's theology of solidarity helps deepen this understanding. Reflecting on the cross in Knowing Christ Crucified, Copeland writes:

“There is no more concrete example of the cost of self-transcending love than the cross of the crucified Jewish Jesus, and it is from the ground beneath his cross that Christian discipleship as solidaristic praxis or compassionate arise and is always judged...A theological understanding of solidarity realizes itself in intelligent and effective compassionate action, in doing, in discipleship” (143).

For Copeland, the cross becomes the ground from which Christian solidarity emerges. We do not simply look at suffering from a safe distance. We are drawn toward it. We become willing to see, to accompany, to act. This gives Jesus's command to keep awake a much more intentional meaning. Keeping awake means refusing to become numb to the suffering around us. It means allowing ourselves to see the people and circumstances that our culture would rather us overlook. It means noticing who is hungry, who is lonely, who is excluded, who is afraid, who is grieving, who is being harmed, who has been pushed to the margins.

And then it means doing something about it. Advent hope is therefore not passive optimism. It is solidaristic hope.

We do not wait for Christ by withdrawing from the world's suffering. We wait for Christ by becoming the kind of people who recognize Christ in the midst of that suffering and responding with compassionate action.

The candles we light during Advent are not only reminders that Jesus is coming. They are invitations to become people who carry light into dark places.

And that brings us to Paul's words in 1 Corinthians. Paul tells the Corinthian church that he gives thanks to God because they have been enriched in Christ, and then he says something incredibly hopeful:

“You are not lacking in any spiritual gift as you wait for the revealing of our Lord Jesus Christ.” As they wait.

Paul acknowledges that the Christian life takes place in the space between Christ's coming and Christ's coming again.

We are a people who wait. We wait as people who have already encountered Christ. We wait as people who have already received God's grace. We wait as people who have already been called into fellowship with Christ.

And then Paul says, "God is faithful; by him you were called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ our Lord."

God is faithful!!! Our hope rests not in our ability to see the future but in the nature of God.

God is faithful while we wait. God is faithful when restoration feels incomplete. God is faithful when we cannot yet see what God is doing. God is faithful when the shadows seem to be winning.

Remember in Isaiah, the people are waiting for God to tear open the heavens and come down. But perhaps part of what Isaiah is learning is that God's work of restoration does not always arrive in the powerful and dramatic way we expect.

Sometimes God comes slowly. Sometimes restoration looks like rebuilding. Sometimes it looks like a community learning again how to worship, how to care for one another, how to practice justice, how to welcome the vulnerable, how to become a people who embody the future they are waiting for.

This the tension of Advent.

We are waiting for God while also becoming the kind of people God is forming us to be.

We wait for restoration, and we participate in restoration.

We wait for justice, and we practice justice.

We wait for peace, and we become peacemakers.

We wait for God's kingdom, and we embody and enflesh God's kingdom in the places where we live.

Jesus has come. Jesus is coming. Jesus will come again.

The Christ who came to Bethlehem comes to us still, in the presence of the Holy Spirit, in the gathered community, at the table, on the road, in the neighbor who needs our attention, and in the places where God's people are participating in the work of restoration.

And one day, Christ will come again and make all things new.

Keeping awake means refusing to look away. It means standing beneath the cross with our eyes open. It means allowing the suffering of the world to move us toward compassionate action. It means waiting for Christ while participating in the work of Christ.

So, on this first Sunday of Advent, the invitation is clear.

We keep awake while waiting with hope. Because the God who came to us in Jesus Christ has not abandoned the world. The God who tore open the heavens in Christ is still at work among us. And the God who has promised to come again is faithful.

So we wait.

Amen.
