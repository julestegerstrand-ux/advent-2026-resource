Isaiah 40:1-11

Isaiah 40 begins a shift in the book of Isaiah. The first 39 chapters largely speak into a period of warning, judgment, and impending destruction. Isaiah 40 opens a new section, often referred to as 2nd Isaiah (Isaiah 40–55). Here, Isaiah is speaking to a people who have already experienced the devastation that earlier chapters warned about. Jerusalem has fallen. The temple has been destroyed. The people have lived through conquest and exile. They have lost their nation, their king, their temple, their freedom, and their familiar way of life.

And now, after all of that, the first words we hear are, “Comfort, O comfort my people, says your God.”

That is a powerful word to speak into this kind of suffering. God does not begin by explaining everything that happened or telling the people to try harder. God begins with comfort. Speak tenderly to Jerusalem. Tell them that their story is not over, that God is not finished with them yet, despite the devastation and destruction.

Isaiah 40 is a text about peace in the aftermath of loss and grief. The people are living in the tension between what they have experienced and what God is promising. They have every reason to wonder whether God has abandoned them. They have every reason to ask whether they have a future. Are they waiting for something? Will God work again in their midst?

Into that uncertainty comes the voice crying in the wilderness: “Prepare the way of the Lord.” The would have reminded those who first heard these words of the Exodus. Just as God once made a way for Israel out of slavery in Egypt, God is now making a way through the wilderness for God's people to return from exile. The highway through the desert is poetic language for a new Exodus, a new act of liberation. The valleys will be lifted, the mountains brought low, the rough places made plain. The God who heard the cries of enslaved people in Egypt is still the God who hears the cries of God's exiled people in Babylon.

An important thing to note is that the people are not being told to find their own way home. God is making the way. Their hope rests not in their ability to control what happens next but in the faithfulness of God.

That makes this particularly fitting for Advent and the theme of peace. Advent is a season of expectation. We wait for something we cannot yet fully see. But Christian waiting is not empty waiting. We wait because we have received a promise. In a Spirituality of Waiting, Henri Nouwen writes, "Waiting, as we see it in the people on the first pages of the Gospel, is waiting with a sense of promise already begun in us. So waiting is never a movement from nothing to something. It is always a movement from 'Zechariah...your wife Elizabeth is to bear you a son.' 'Mary...Listen! You are to conceive and bear a son.' (Luke 1. 13, 31) People who wait have received a promise that allows them to wait. They have received something that is at work in them, like a seed that has started to grow. This is very important. We can only really wait if what we are waiting for has something more." The people of Isaiah 40 can wait because God has spoken. Their circumstances have not yet changed, but God's promise gives them a reason to believe that their circumstances are not the end of their story.

Isaiah gives us a beautiful image in verse 11: “He will feed his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms, and carry them in his bosom, and gently lead the mother sheep.” God who has been described throughout Isaiah as powerful and mighty is now revealed as bending down to gather vulnerable lambs into God's arms. God's faithfulness is revealed not only in power but in tenderness, presence, and care.

The text does not promise that suffering will disappear. It does not deny the brokenness of the world or pretend that everything is already as it should be. Instead, it gives us a different kind of hope: God has not abandoned us. God is still making a way. The future is peace.

That is the hope of Advent. We wait because we have already caught glimpses of what we are waiting for. We have experienced moments of peace, in a song, around a table, in the embrace of a loved one, in a community gathered together. Those glimpses do not mean the world is already whole. They remind us that peace is possible because God is still at work.

So we wait. We hope. We long for something more. And Advent reminds us that the God who came to us in the vulnerability of a baby in a manger is still the God who comes alongside us in the wilderness, gathers us into God's arms, and makes a way where we cannot yet see one. Thanks be to God.
