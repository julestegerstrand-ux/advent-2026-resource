2 Samuel 7:1-11, 16

2 Samuel 7 comes during a significant time in the story of Israel. David has become king over a united Israel, Jerusalem has been established as his capital, and the ark of God has been brought into the city. David now lives in a house of cedar while the ark remains in a tent. His desire to build a temple seems, at first, like an appropriate act of devotion. Yet through Nathan the prophet, God turns David's plan upside down. David wants to build a house for God, but God promises to build a house for David.

Generally scholars understand 2 Samuel as part of the larger Deuteronomistic History. This is the biblical narrative from Deuteronomy through 2 Kings, which took shape through a lengthy process of collecting, editing, and preserving older traditions. Many would suggest that it came together fully during Israel's exile or shortly afterward in the sixth century BC. Its audience was living in the shadow of Jerusalem's destruction and the loss of the Davidic monarchy. In that context, the promise of an enduring house for David in 2 Samuel 7 would have carried powerful theological significance. The passage asks what God's faithfulness means when circumstances seem to suggest that God's promises have failed.

In terms of where this story fits into the larger narrative of 1 and 2 Samuel, this chapter is a major turning point in 2 Samuel. It shifts attention from what David will accomplish for God to what God promises to accomplish through David and his descendants. The passage also establishes the Davidic covenant, which becomes an important theme throughout the Old Testament and eventually shapes early Christian understandings of Jesus as the promised descendant of David.

The passage reminds us that God’s purposes cannot be reduced to our plans, even when our plans appear faithful. In the late 500s AD, Gregory the Great, the bishop of Rome and one of the major theologians of the early medieval church, noticed this same shift from human plans to God’s purposes in his reflection on Nathan. He uses this passage to reflect on the unpredictable work of prophecy. Gregory has his deacon Peter ask questions, creating a dialogue between the two. Gregory writes:

“The Spirit of prophecy does not enlighten the minds of the prophets constantly, Peter. We read in sacred Scripture that the Holy Spirit breathes when he pleases. For example, when King David asked whether he could build a temple, the prophet Nathan gave his consent, but later had to withdraw it” (Dialogue 2.21, FC 39:88–89).

He explains that Nathan initially approved David’s desire to build the temple but later had to withdraw that approval when God gave him a different word. Nathan, the prophet, had to remain open to God’s Spirit redirecting his understanding.

Gregory’s reflection points us toward something at the heart of Advent. God’s purposes exceed our plans, and God’s grace often moves toward us before we recognize what God is doing. This makes 2 Samuel 7 a profound text for the Fourth Sunday of Advent, the Sunday of Love. Advent reminds us that God's love is not simply our love for God. God's love is first God's gracious movement toward us. In the Church of the Nazarene as Wesleyans we often refer to this as prevenient grace. David wants to do something for God, but God responds by promising what God will do for David. The initiative belongs to God. Everything we do is in response to God's grace. We don't initiate prayer. We respond to God's invitation to pray. We don’t initiate our salvation. God reaches toward us with grace before we ever reach toward God. We don’t initiate God’s love. We receive and respond to the love God has already extended to us. We don’t even initiate our search for God. God is already seeking us, drawing us, and making a way toward us. Our faithfulness, then, is not about trying to get God to notice us or earn God’s favor. It is our response to the God who has already moved toward us in holy love.

Eugene Peterson gives language to this same truth in a deeply personal way. In his book Run With the Horses: The Quest for Life at its Best, Eugene Peterson argues, "Long before we got interested in the subject of God, God subjected us to the most intensive and searching knowledge. Before it ever crossed our minds that God might be important, God singled us out as important. Before we were formed in the womb, God knew us. We are known before we know" (39). David thought he was coming up with a plan for God, but God reminds him that long before David made his plans, God had already been at work in his life. And the same is true for us. We are known before we know, loved before we love, and sought before we ever think to seek God.

And that is where, I believe, Advent leads us. God does not wait for humanity to construct the perfect house, create the perfect congregation, or make ourselves worthy of God's presence. God simply comes to us. The promise of David's house eventually points beyond David to Jesus, the one through whom God's steadfast love takes flesh and dwells among us. In Christ, we affirm that God does not wait for us to make our way to God. God makes God's way to us, first.

Love, then, on this fourth Sunday of Advent is not sentimental affection or attraction. It is the persistent, covenantal love of God who keeps God's promises, even when (and perhaps especially when) God's people cannot yet see how those promises will be fulfilled. Amen.
