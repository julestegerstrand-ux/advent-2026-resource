Theological Reflections for Preaching and Teaching on Christmas Eve-The Incarnation.

Christmas Eve (and the entire season of Christmas) is an ideal time to reflect on the incarnation of God, in Jesus the Christ. This holy night gathers us around the mystery that the eternal Word became flesh and dwelt among us. The birth of Jesus is not simply the beginning of a story, but the embodiment of God’s redemptive plan entering human history in the most intimate way possible.

In the incarnation, God entered a human body as Jesus the Christ. African Church Father Saint Athanasius of Alexandria maintained that Jesus was fully God and fully human during the Arian controversy which led to the council of Nicea. Athanasius articulated that Jesus was God in the flesh. Athanasius believed that prior to Jesus, humanity required redemption, and only God can bring that redemption. Therefore, Jesus must be fully God. To break the power of sin on humanity, Jesus also must be fully human. Therefore, Athanasius reasoned that Jesus was fully God and fully human. This union of divinity and humanity is the heart of the Christmas message: heaven and earth meet in the manger.

Athanasius’s teachings have been affirmed as orthodox within Christianity. Athanasius stated: What then was God to do? Or what should be done, except to renew again the “in the image,” so that through its human beings would be able once again to know him? But how could this have occurred except by the coming of the very image of God, our Savior Jesus Christ? For neither by human beings was it possible, since they were created “in the image”; but neither by angels, for they were not even images. So the Word of God came himself, in order that he being the image of the Father, the human being “in the image” might be recreated. It could not, again, have been done any other way, without death and corruption being utterly destroyed. So he rightly took a mortal body, that in it death might henceforth be destroyed utterly and human beings be renewed again according to the image.

Humans were and are created in the image of God. Sin corrupted God’s image in humanity. According to Athanasius, God assumed a human body as Jesus the Christ to destroy death and renew humanity according to God’s image. Another translation of this ancient text says, “The Word of God came in his own Person.” God, in Christ, entered a body for us and our salvation. On Christmas Eve, we remember that this salvation began not in a palace but in a humble stable, among ordinary people and fragile circumstances. The incarnation reveals that God’s redemptive work meets us in the ordinary, in the flesh, and in the vulnerability of human life.

If Jesus became fully human and did not just appear human as many Docetists, Apollinarians, and Gnostics have erroneously argued, then the incarnation of God in Christ reveals the importance of the redemption of bodies. Athanasius continued, “For he was incarnate that we might be made God; and he manifested himself through a body that we might receive an idea of the invisible Father; and he endured the insults of human beings, that we might inherit incorruptibility.” In other words, the incarnation affirms that salvation is not an escape from the body but the renewal and resurrection of it.

Athanasius is not literally saying that humans become gods. Instead, he is suggesting that in the incarnation the Infinite One (God) becomes finite (human) so that finite beings might become infinite. One Catholic author, Karlo Broussard also supports this when he says, “the phrase, ‘that we might become God’ is better translated as ‘that we might be deified.’ The Greek word for ‘deified,’ theopoiethomen, has the connotation of participation in rather than becoming God.” According to Athanasius, God entered a body to empower humanity to participate more fully in God, thus redeeming the whole human, both bodies and souls.

Therefore, Christmas Eve emphasizes the salvation of souls and the liberation of bodies for they are eternally interconnected because of the incarnation of Jesus. On this holy night, we celebrate that God has drawn near, not as an abstract idea but as a living presence. The light that shines in the darkness is not merely a metaphor but the real illumination of divine love entering our world. The cry of the newborn Christ is the cry of divine solidarity with human suffering.

Christmas is the annual proclamation that God is with us, Emmanuel. The manger scene becomes the theological centerpiece of God’s redemptive love: the infinite made intimate, the divine made visible, the holy made human. Athanasius’ theology helps us see that Christmas is not only a celebration of Jesus’ birth but of God’s action of making us more like God. Each candle lit and each carol sung proclaims that through Christ, our humanity is renewed and our bodies made sacred once more. The incarnation transforms Christmas from a sentimental story into a divine revolution. For the Creator entering creation makes all things new. In Jesus, heaven touches earth, and we are invited to share in the very life of God. Amen.
