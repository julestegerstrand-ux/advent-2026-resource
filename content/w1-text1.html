Isaiah 64:1-9

Isaiah 64 comes from what is often called 3rd Isaiah (Isaiah 56–66). Scholars believe this section of Isaiah was written after the Jews had returned from exile. They have returned to Jerusalem "the City of Peace," but the return has not produced the healing that they envisioned. The temple and city are not what they once were, the community is fractured, and the promises of restoration seem frustratingly incomplete. They are home, but they are still waiting for God to make things right.

That context matters for the season of Advent. Hope is not ignoring the reality in front of us, nor is it denying the pain of the present. Isaiah gives voice to a people who have experienced trauma followed by disappointment and yet continue to hope that God will act: “O that you would tear open the heavens and come down” (64:1). The prayer is bold, perhaps even desperate. They are asking God to show up and transform their circumstances.

But there is also a prophetic challenge underneath the prayer. The problem is not only that God needs to come back. God's people need to return to faithfully following the covenant. Throughout this section of Isaiah, the prophet challenges a community that has returned from exile to examine what kind of people they are becoming. Worship cannot be separated from justice, compassion, and faithfulness. They cannot simply rebuild the temple, resume religious practices, and assume everything is fine. Authentic worship has to be embodied in the way they treat one another, particularly the vulnerable and marginalized.

That is why verse 5 is so important: “You meet those who gladly do right, those who remember you in your ways.” The hope for God's presence is connected to the community's own faithfulness. Isaiah is not offering a simplistic formula. It is not if we behave, God will bless us. Instead, I believe Isaiah is insisting that hope in God must produce a particular kind of people. If we are waiting for God to come, we should also be asking what it means for us to live faithfully while we wait.

The language of verses 6–7 becomes confessional. “We have all become like one who is unclean and all our righteous deeds are like a filthy cloth. We all fade like a leaf, and our iniquities, like the wind, take us away. There is no one who calls on your name, or attempts to take hold of you.” Isaiah is proclaiming to the community that their own righteousness is woefully inadequate. They have wandered, and they need God not only to fix their circumstances but to restore their relationship with God.

And then comes a turn in verses 8–9. “Yet, O LORD, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand.” Even after confession, the prayer ends not in despair but in relationship, in hope. They appeal to who God is. God is their Father. They are God's people. They remain the work of God's hands.

That makes Isaiah 64 a powerful text for the first Sunday of Advent and the theme of hope. Advent begins with Isaiah revealing to us a people waiting in the darkness, longing for God to tear open the heavens and come down. But, we must remember that our hope is not passive waiting. We wait for God by becoming the kind of people who embody God's coming kingdom. We worship authentically. We practice justice. We remember the covenant. We make room for our neighbors. We live as people who believe God is still at work. Maybe that is what holiness is. Not getting our lives perfectly together, but allowing God to reshape how we worship, do justice, and live together. As we wait and hope for God to tear open the heavens, we respond by tearing down the walls that keep people from belonging.

And perhaps that is the tension of Advent. We are waiting for God to come, while also recognizing that God is already calling us to live faithfully in the worlds we inhabit. The hope is that God has not abandoned the work of God's hands, and neither has God abandoned us. Thanks be to God.
